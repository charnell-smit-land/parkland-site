What Should a Small Business Fix First?

By Charnell Smith-Landry, Founder · November 4, 2026 · 3 min read

The short answer

Fix the problem that happens most often and that your customers can feel. Not the loudest problem, and not the newest one. A two-week tally of what keeps going wrong usually makes the answer obvious.

Why the Loudest Problem Is Rarely the Right One

Every owner has a list. The website feels dated, a team member keeps missing details, the software is clunky, reviews could be better. When everything feels urgent, the problem that gets fixed is usually the one that caused the most stress this week.

That is a feeling, not a pattern. The problem worth fixing first is the one that keeps repeating and keeps touching customers, because every repeat quietly costs you trust, time or a sale.

Two Questions Sort the Whole List

Ask two things about every problem on your list. How often does it happen? And how close is it to the customer? Put the answers on a simple grid and your priorities sort themselves.

Problems in the top right are your Fix First list. Frequent but internal problems get scheduled. Rare problems customers feel get watched. Rare internal problems can usually be let go for now.

For exampleA gift boutique was set on redesigning its website. A two-week tally showed the real repeat problem was online orders shipping a day late, which showed up in reviews and support emails every week. Fixing the packing routine did more for sales than the redesign would have.

The Move

  1. Keep a Two-Week TallyEvery time something goes wrong, write one line: what happened and who noticed. Ask your team to add to it. Two weeks is enough to see what repeats.
  2. Place Each Problem on the GridCount how often each one showed up, then mark whether customers felt it. Be honest about the second part. If a customer waited, called or complained, they felt it.
  3. Fix One Thing CompletelyPick the single problem furthest into the top right and fix it all the way before starting the next. Half-fixed problems come back.

Pattern Check

  • Could you name the problem that happened most often last month?
  • Have you fixed something recently that customers never noticed?
  • Does your team agree on what the biggest repeat problem is?

If you hesitated on any of these, a two-week tally will tell you more than another planning meeting.
